Cycle count program example: ABC count frequency, accuracy math and an AI prompt

Short answerCount A items most often (for example monthly), B items quarterly and C items once or twice a year, then add extra counts in high-handling areas such as returns. Daily workload = total counts per year ÷ working days. Score each count against a tolerance by class, and report location accuracy together with absolute (not net) variance value.

Fictional exampleExample Valve Co., its warehouse, items and every number below are made up. Don't paste real supplier pricing or customer data into an AI tool; see the data-safety checklist.

Your system says there are 60 on the shelf. The shelf says zero. Somewhere in the building there is a perfectly good case of parts, sitting in the wrong slot and quietly turning into a stockout. A cycle count program is how you find it in weeks instead of at the annual physical count. This page sets one up for a fictional 3,000-SKU warehouse: how often to count each class, what the daily workload is, how to score six count results, and an AI prompt that does the analysis with you.

The problem is common. In a study of nearly 370,000 inventory records across 37 stores of one retailer, DeHoratius and Raman (Management Science, 2008) found 65% of the records inaccurate, and named inventory auditing practices among the factors that reduce the error. Bad records also feed straight into your safety stock math, which assumes the starting number is right.

What is cycle counting?

Cycle counting means counting a small set of locations or items every working day, on a schedule, so the whole inventory gets checked several times a year without shutting the building for a wall-to-wall count. Each count is compared with the system quantity, variances are researched and corrected, and the results show you where records go wrong and why.

How often should you count each item?

Most programs count by ABC class: the A items that carry most of the value or movement get counted most often. Example Valve Co. uses this plan:

ClassSKUsCounts per SKU per yearCounts per year
A20012 (monthly)2,400
B6004 (quarterly)2,400
C2,2002 (twice a year)4,400
Total3,0009,200

Over 250 working days that is 9,200 ÷ 250 = 36.8, so about 37 counts a day. At an assumed 4 minutes per count, 37 × 4 = 148 minutes, or about 2.5 labor hours a day. Run that math before you promise anyone monthly counts on everything.

Value isn't the only reason to count more often. Areas with a lot of handling, such as returns, singles and overflow, tend to produce more errors. ASICS America, for example, moved two such areas from twice-a-year counts to monthly drone counts and reports finding misplaced inventory about 60% faster (issue #16, from the Corvus Robotics case study).

Location accuracy is 3 of 6 counts within tolerance, or 50%. The net variance is −$280.00, but the absolute variance is $50 + $0 + $20 + $72 + $12 + $150 = $304.00. Track the absolute number: the +$12 overage on C-310 doesn't cancel the missing C-377, it is usually a second error. And the $150 miss on a cheap C item matters more than its value suggests. Its case turned up two locations away, so the system showed stock that pickers couldn't find, and every order for it would have been short.

What should you check after each count?

  • Research before you adjust. Check open picks, receipts not yet put away and nearby locations before you post an adjustment.
  • Sort by cause, not just by item. Wrong location, unit-of-measure mix-up, unrecorded scrap and receiving errors each need a different fix.
  • Report hit rate and absolute variance value together. Accuracy by count shows process health; absolute value shows where the money is.
  • Move frequency to where the errors are. If one area misses three times as often, count it more often, even if its items are cheap.
  • Watch for stock held back from sale. If sales or e-commerce hold back units from availability because they don't trust the count, that is lost revenue the count program can win back. Stock that keeps failing counts is also a candidate for the excess and obsolete review.

Do it with AI

You are an inventory control analyst reviewing cycle count results. Inputs, all anonymized, one row per count: location, item code, ABC class, system quantity, counted quantity, unit cost, storage area, and the tolerance for the class (for example A 0%, B 2%, C 5%).
[PASTE TABLE]
1) For each row, calculate variance (counted minus system), variance %, whether it is within tolerance, and variance value (variance x unit cost). Show your math for the first row.
2) Calculate location accuracy (counts within tolerance divided by total counts), net variance value and absolute variance value, overall and by storage area.
3) Group the misses by likely cause (wrong location, unit-of-measure error, receiving error, unrecorded scrap or unknown) and say what information would confirm each cause.
4) Suggest which storage areas or classes should be counted more often, and estimate the extra counts per month.
Output: a table with columns location, item, variance, variance %, within tolerance, variance value, likely cause; then a short summary by storage area. Keep it under 350 words. Use only the numbers I give. List any missing or inconsistent data instead of guessing.

What to check: recompute one row by hand or in the template, confirm each likely cause on the floor before you post an adjustment, and agree tolerances and count frequencies with your supply chain manager and Finance. FAQ

What is a cycle count program?

A schedule for counting a small set of items or locations every working day, so the whole inventory is checked several times a year without a full shutdown count.

How often should A, B and C items be counted?

A common starting point is A monthly, B quarterly and C once or twice a year. In the example on this page, that is 9,200 counts a year, about 37 a day.

How do you calculate inventory accuracy from cycle counts?

Divide the counts within tolerance by total counts. In the example, 3 of 6 counts are within tolerance, so location accuracy is 50%.

Why use absolute variance instead of net?

Overages and shortages cancel out in the net number. In the example, the net variance is −$280 but the absolute variance is $304.

Can ChatGPT analyze cycle count results?

It can do the variance math and group misses by likely cause from an anonymized table. Confirm causes on the floor before you adjust any records.
