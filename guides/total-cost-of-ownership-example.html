Total cost of ownership example: a 3-year offshore vs domestic supplier comparison

Short answerTotal cost of ownership is everything a part costs you over the decision period, not just its price: price, freight, duty and brokerage (landed cost), plus the carrying cost of inventory in transit and safety stock, quality losses, supplier management, and one-time costs like tooling. Multiply per-unit costs by volume and years, then add one-time costs.

Fictional exampleExample Lighting Co., Suppliers X and Y and every price, rate and duty below are made up. The 4% duty is fictional. This page covers purchased parts (supplier TCO), not car or software TCO. Don't paste real NDA pricing into an AI tool; see the data-safety checklist.

Total cost of ownership is what a part will cost you to own over a decision period. A should-cost model asks what it should cost to make—see the should-cost model example. To put mismatched quotes on the same basis first (Incoterms, currency, MOQ), see the RFQ bid comparison example; this page starts where that one stops.

What does total cost of ownership include in procurement?

LineWhat it covers
PriceUnit price from the quote
FreightCost to move the part to your door
DutyDepends on HS code and origin—get it from your broker or the HTS (see HS code classification with AI)
Brokerage / handlingCustoms broker and related fees
Inventory carryingLanded × weeks you own the stock ÷ 52 × carrying rate
QualityDefect rate × cost per defect that reaches the line
Supplier management / expeditesOngoing effort and rush shipments
One-timeTooling, qualification, transition

Optional extras: payment-terms cash cost, currency risk, IP risk. The Reshoring Initiative's free TCO Estimator covers 30 cost and risk factors; use as many lines as your decision needs.

What's the TCO formula?

TCO = (per-unit recurring costs × annual volume × years) + one-time costs. Then TCO per unit = TCO ÷ total units over the horizon. Always state the horizon—one-time costs look three times bigger over one year than over three.

Worked example: a die-cast housing, 20,000 a year for 3 years

Example Lighting Co. chooses a source for a die-cast housing: 20,000 pcs/yr for 3 years (60,000 pcs). Carrying rate 25%/yr (the same fictional rate as the RFQ page). Cost per defective unit reaching the line: $20.

Result: Supplier X's price is $1.40 (17.5%) lower, but Supplier Y's 3-year TCO is $13,363 lower. Price is the biggest line; the decision is made in the smaller ones (weeks of stock, defects, tooling).

The Reshoring Initiative states that price-only decisions often result "in a 20 to 30 percent miscalculation of actual offshoring costs" (their claim, not a measured statistic from this site). The example above is one way that gap shows up.

What would change the answer?

At a 0% duty, Supplier X's 3-year TCO falls to about $605,135 and it wins by roughly $7,130. The break-even duty is about 1.4% of the $8.00 price. The decision sits close to the line—check the real duty rate before you decide, and recheck when tariffs change (see #tariffs). Lead time and defect rate move the answer the same way: feed them from your supplier scorecard, not from a guess.

Do it with AI

You are a sourcing analyst. Compare these suppliers on total cost of ownership over [3] years at [20,000] units/year. For each supplier I'll give: price, freight, duty rate, brokerage, transit weeks, safety-stock weeks, defect rate, cost per defect, supplier-management cost per unit, and one-time costs [PASTE]. Use carrying rate [25%]. Calculate landed cost, carrying cost (landed × weeks ÷ 52 × rate), quality cost, recurring cost per unit, 3-year TCO and TCO per unit. Show every formula and number in a table. Then find the duty rate, defect rate or transit time at which the ranking flips. Don't look up or invent duty rates; use only the rates I give and list anything missing.

What to check: get the duty rate from your broker or the HTS for the actual HS code and origin; confirm the carrying rate with Finance; check the defect rate against scorecard data; recompute one column by hand.

FAQ

What does total cost of ownership include in procurement?

Price, freight, duty, brokerage, inventory carrying, quality losses, supplier management and one-time costs such as tooling, over a stated horizon.

What is the TCO formula?

TCO = (per-unit recurring costs × annual volume × years) + one-time costs. TCO per unit = TCO ÷ total units.

What's an example of total cost of ownership in procurement?

The offshore vs domestic housing example on this page: Supplier X's price is 17.5% lower, but Supplier Y wins on 3-year TCO by about $13,300 once carrying, quality and tooling are included.

What's the difference between landed cost and total cost of ownership?

Landed cost is the cost to get the part to your door. TCO adds carrying, quality, management and one-time costs over time.

Is there a TCO template in Excel?

Yes. The CSV on this page opens in Excel or Sheets with formulas filled in. The Reshoring Initiative also offers a free online estimator covering 30 cost factors.

How do tariffs change TCO?

They change the duty line. In the example, the ranking flips at about 1.4% duty. Recheck when rates change; see our tariffs coverage for news.
