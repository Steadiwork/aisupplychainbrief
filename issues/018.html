The cheap chips AI is crowding out

Inside today: AI demand squeezes the cheap chips · Hitachi's AI agents chase delivery dates · Command Alkon's read-only AI analyst · NTT's robot AI predicts load collapse

Nexperia, a maker of low-cost chips used mainly in cars and industrial equipment, is "totally oversubscribed," its interim CEO told Reuters on November 10, as suppliers shift capacity toward AI data centers. Parts that cost cents and always showed up now belong on your shortage watch list.

Top storyManufacturingSupply chain for AI

1. AI data centers are crowding out the cheap chips factories run on

"Totally oversubscribed." That's how interim CEO Stefan Tilger described Nexperia, a maker of low-cost chips used mainly in cars and industrial equipment, to Reuters at the Electronica trade fair in Munich on November 10. He said some competitors have moved capacity away from these commodity chips as AI data centers and robotics absorb supply, after years of thin investment in the technology behind diodes, transistors and other basic parts. A day earlier, STMicroelectronics CEO Jean-Marc Chery told a CEO panel, "We see many bottlenecks," and said some foundries and packaging firms "are disengaging" from mainstream technology. Costs are moving too. Auto supplier Aumovio said on November 5 that higher memory costs weigh on its 2026, and its interim head of finance told analysts he expects a gross earnings hit of about €1 billion in 2027, driven mainly by chip inflation, Quartz reported, citing Bloomberg. Why it matters: the parts that never made your shortage list, because they cost cents and always arrived, now compete with AI buyers for the same factory time.

So what for materials managersFind the low-cost chips on your bills of material that have one source or thin coverage, and ask those suppliers now whether the parts are on allocation.

What to do next
  1. Filter your bills of material for diodes, transistors, memory and other mature-node chips, and list each part's suppliers, quoted lead time now versus six months ago, and weeks of stock on hand and on order.
  2. Ask each supplier and distributor in writing whether those parts are on allocation, whether lead times or prices changed in the last six months, and whether any last-time-buy notices are coming.
  3. Where coverage is shorter than the quoted lead time and there is only one approved source, start an approved-alternate review with engineering and raise the part at your next S&OP meeting.
You are a materials analyst checking supply risk on low-cost electronic components. Inputs, anonymized (codes instead of supplier and part names), one row per part: part code, part type (diode, transistor, capacitor, memory, other), supplier code, number of approved sources, quoted lead time now (weeks), quoted lead time 6 months ago (weeks), on-hand quantity, open PO quantity due within the lead time, average weekly usage, unit price now, unit price 6 months ago, and whether the supplier says the part is on allocation (yes/no/unknown).
[PASTE TABLE]
1) For each part, calculate weeks of coverage ((on hand + open PO due within lead time) ÷ average weekly usage) and the gap to the quoted lead time (coverage minus lead time). Show your math for the first row.
2) Calculate the 6-month change in lead time and in unit price, in percent.
3) Flag parts with coverage shorter than lead time, only one approved source, allocation marked yes or unknown, or a lead time up more than 25%.
4) Rank the flagged parts: negative gap first, then single source, then lead-time change.
5) For the top 10, suggest one next step each (ask for allocation status, propose a buffer order for approval, start an approved-alternate review, or watch) with a one-line reason.
Output: a table with columns part, type, supplier, sources, coverage (weeks), lead time (weeks), gap (weeks), lead-time change, price change, flags, next step. Keep it under 300 words. Use only the data I give. List any missing or inconsistent data instead of guessing.

Tip of the DayFor Planners

Have AI check supplier acknowledgments against your need dates

An order acknowledgment that quietly moves the date, quantity or price is a plan change nobody told you about. Export open PO lines and the matching acknowledgment lines, swap supplier and part names for codes, and have your approved AI tool list every mismatch and every PO line with no acknowledgment at all. You get a short exception list to fix before the plan runs, instead of reading 200 confirmations, which nobody will miss doing.

You are a supply planner checking supplier order acknowledgments against open purchase orders. Inputs, anonymized (codes instead of supplier and part names). Table A, open PO lines: PO number, line, supplier code, part code, ordered quantity, unit price, need date. Table B, acknowledgment lines: PO number, line, acknowledged quantity, acknowledged unit price, promised date, acknowledgment date.

[PASTE TABLE B]
1) Match lines on PO number and line. List PO lines with no acknowledgment, and acknowledgment lines with no matching PO line.
2) For matched lines, flag quantity differences, unit price differences and promised dates later than the need date, with the difference in units, dollars and days.
3) Rank the flags: promised date later than need date first (most days late first), then quantity short, then price higher.
4) Draft one short, neutral question to each supplier code that covers all of its flagged lines.
Output: a table with columns PO, line, supplier, part, issue, PO value, acknowledged value, difference; then the supplier questions. Keep it under 300 words. Use only the data I give. List any missing or inconsistent data instead of guessing.

ProcurementAI in supply chain

2. Hitachi puts AI agents on quote comparison and delivery-date follow-up

Up to 65% less time on selected delivery-management tasks. That's Hitachi's estimate from using its new TWX-21 Procurement AI Service inside its own group, announced November 9, and it says results will vary by workflow. The agents compare quotes, place orders, spot likely late deliveries early and support delivery-date talks with suppliers, while procurement staff make the final decisions.

So what for materials managersBefore an AI agent chases late deliveries for you, write down your own rules: which suppliers get a call, which get an email, and when a date slip comes to you.

PlanningAI in supply chain

3. Command Alkon's new AI: a read-only analyst plus an assistant that can wait for approval

Read-only by design. That's how Command Alkon describes Analyst, half of Command Intelligence, the AI tool it made available on November 12 to producers of concrete, asphalt and aggregates that use its Command Cloud software. Analyst answers plain-language questions, such as which materials need replenishment, and shows the evidence behind each answer; Assistant handles routine work like flagging mispriced tickets and can pause for an employee's approval before anything is written to the system.

So what for operations and plant managersWhen an AI tool can both answer and act, open the read-only side to everyone first and limit the acting side to a few approved, logged tasks.

WarehousingAI in supply chain

4. NTT's robot AI predicts a load collapse before the arm moves

From 73.1% to 92.3%. That's how far a small robot's success rate rose in NTT's shelf-stacking test when a new physics-based AI model predicted, before each move, whether the load would collapse or an item would drop, the company said on November 11. NTT plans field trials with robotics company Mujin, starting with airport baggage loading and unloading, and then wants to take the technology into logistics and warehousing.

So what for warehouse managersWhen a robot vendor quotes success rates, ask whether they came from a lab test or from live trailers and mixed cartons like yours.
