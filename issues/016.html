The inventory you own but can't sell

Inside today: ASICS drones find lost stock 60% faster · Infineon's €10B in power-chip deals · DHL takes AI orchestration live · Path Robotics raises $300M for AI welding

ASICS America used to hold back 12 to 15 of every 100 units from its online marketplace feeds because it didn't fully trust the count. Monthly drone counts in its riskiest storage areas let it publish the full feed. The cheapest inventory to add, it turns out, is the inventory you already have.

Four stories from the last few days, from a warehouse that stopped hiding its own stock to power chips booked years ahead, across warehousing, procurement and manufacturing. Every item links to the original source; summaries are our own.

1. ASICS finds misplaced stock about 60% faster with nightly drone counts

Twice a year became monthly. ASICS America says autonomous inventory drones from Corvus Robotics now count two of the highest-risk storage areas in its 855,000-square-foot distribution center in Byhalia, Mississippi, every month, a sixfold jump in counting frequency. ASICS reports recovering misplaced inventory about 60% faster and moving about 200 hours a month of counting labor into research and exception work. In one three-month stretch the drones found 26 misplaced cases holding more than 200 pairs of shoes. The figures come from ASICS and Corvus, announced November 10; neither disclosed a price or fleet size. The part supply chain managers should notice is the revenue side. Before the drones, ASICS held back 12 to 15 units of every 100 from its marketplace availability feeds because of fulfillment risk. It now publishes the full feed and sells that product at full price. Why it matters: inventory you can't trust is inventory you can't sell, and the fix started with counting the messiest areas more often, not the whole building.

So what for supply chain managersFind the storage areas where your counts miss most and where stock is held back from sale, then count those more often first.

What to do next
  1. Pull last quarter's cycle count results and rank storage areas or location types by miss rate and by the value of the variances.
  2. Ask sales or e-commerce how many units they hold back from availability feeds or promise dates because they don't trust the count, and in which areas.
  3. Pick the worst one or two areas and test a higher count frequency (manual, scanner or drone) for 60 days before you decide on automation.
You are an inventory control analyst deciding where more frequent cycle counts would pay off. Inputs, anonymized, one row per count: storage area or location type (Area A/B/C), item code, system quantity, counted quantity, unit cost, count date, and whether the item is held back from sale or promise dates (yes/no).
[PASTE TABLE]
1) For each area, calculate the miss rate (counts where system and counted quantity differ, divided by total counts) and the absolute variance value (sum of |counted - system| x unit cost).
2) Rank the areas by absolute variance value, then by miss rate.
3) For the top three areas, name the most common variance pattern (shortage, overage, item found in a wrong location) and flag any items also marked as held back from sale.
4) Suggest a count frequency for each top area (weekly, monthly or quarterly) and estimate the counts per month it adds.
Output: a table with columns area, counts, miss rate, absolute variance value, main pattern, held-back items, suggested frequency, counts per month. Keep it under 300 words. Use only the numbers I give. List any missing or inconsistent data instead of guessing.

Tip of the DayFor Supply chain managers

Have AI lay out a messy decision as a one-page options memo

When a decision lands on your desk at 4:45 on a Friday, the slow part is laying out the options fairly, not the typing. Paste the facts you have, with supplier, customer and site names swapped for codes, and ask your approved AI tool for options, costs, risks and what each one needs from other teams. It drafts the memo; you check every number against your own data and make the call.

You are an analyst drafting a one-page decision memo for a supply chain manager. Inputs, anonymized (codes instead of supplier, customer and site names): the decision to make, the deadline, the facts and numbers available, constraints (budget, capacity, contract terms) and who is affected.
[PASTE FACTS]
Decision: [DECISION IN ONE SENTENCE]  Deadline: [DATE]
1) Restate the decision and the deadline in one line.
2) Lay out 2 to 4 realistic options, including doing nothing.
3) For each option, give the cost or savings (only from the numbers given), the service and risk impact, what it needs from other teams, and whether it can be reversed.
4) Recommend one option and say what new information would change the recommendation.
Output: a short memo with one heading per step, plus a table with columns option, cost impact, service impact, main risk, needs from others, reversible (yes/no). Keep it under 350 words. Use anonymized data only. List any missing or inconsistent data instead of guessing.

2. Infineon expects data-center power chip sales to nearly double, backed by over €10 billion in capacity deals

Over €10 billion. That's the combined revenue volume of multi-year supply and capacity reservation agreements, signed or still under negotiation, that Infineon says customers have lined up for its data-center power products. The chipmaker said on November 10 that revenue from power supplies for AI and conventional data centers should reach close to €4 billion in fiscal 2027, up from €2.2 billion. CEO Jochen Hanebeck said Infineon could not meet all customer requests in fiscal 2026, even after ramping production.

So what for buyersIf you buy power semiconductors for industrial or automotive products, ask your distributor or supplier rep whether data-center reservations are changing allocation or lead times on your parts.

WarehousingAI in supply chain

3. DHL Supply Chain moves AI orchestration into live warehouses

More than 9,000 robots, and now a layer meant to run them as one team. DHL Supply Chain said on November 9 that, after a proof of concept, it is deploying AI orchestration in live warehouse operations as a digital control center connecting site data, employees and automated equipment. It is also piloting adaptive robots that work toward a goal instead of following a fixed route. DHL did not publish throughput or cost results for the orchestration layer.

So what for warehouse managersIf your 3PL pitches AI orchestration this peak, ask which site runs it live and what throughput or labor numbers it measured there.

ManufacturingAI in supply chain

4. Path Robotics raises $300 million to take AI welding beyond the weld

$300 million at a $2.45 billion valuation. Path Robotics, which builds AI-guided welding robots, announced the Series E led by Georgian on November 10. Its Obsidian model watches parts warp from heat during welding and adjusts as it goes, which is where robots running a fixed program tend to struggle, SiliconANGLE reported. CEO Andy Lonsberry said manufacturers that need more capacity "can't get there with traditional automation," and the company plans to take on the tasks before and after welding next.

So what for operations and plant managersIf welding is a bottleneck in your supply base, ask your fabrication suppliers whether AI-guided cells are in their capacity plans and how much part variation they handle.
