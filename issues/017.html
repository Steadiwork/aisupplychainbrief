The one part that sets the lead time

Inside today: SpaceX builds a turbine-blade foundry · Infor puts Claude on approved POs · project44's AI analyst scores 10 of 10 · Freightos drafts freight quotes with AI

Turbine suppliers tell SpaceX that gas-turbine blades and vanes arrive 12 to 18 months after the rest of the turbine, Elon Musk said on the company's October 20 earnings call. So SpaceX is building its own casting foundry in Texas. When one part sets your lead time, ordering everything else sooner doesn't help.

Four stories across manufacturing, planning and logistics. The top story goes back to an August announcement, with new detail from October earnings calls; the other three are from this week. Every item links to the original source; summaries are our own.

1. SpaceX moves turbine blade casting in-house to get around a 12-to-18-month wait

Everything but the blades and vanes can ship 12 to 18 months sooner. That's what turbine suppliers tell SpaceX, Elon Musk said on the company's October 20 earnings call. His answer, confirmed on X on August 29, is a SpaceX foundry in Bastrop, Texas, that will cast those parts in-house for the gas turbines that power its AI data centers. SpaceX job postings describe a foundry still being built and staffed, and no production date is public. Analysts cited by Quartz say a new plant like this takes at least four years, and its vacuum furnaces take more than two years to procure. Meanwhile Howmet, which Quartz says holds roughly half the global market for these turbine parts, reported gas turbine revenue up 45% in the third quarter. TechCrunch noted that gas turbines already running at data centers face federal lawsuits and health studies over emissions. Why it matters: one part sets the whole lead time, and SpaceX's response is to try to own that part rather than order the rest sooner.

So what for buyersFind the one part that sets your lead time, and ask what happens to your supply if a big customer starts making it in-house or locks up the capacity.

What to do next
  1. List your top 10 assemblies by lead time and name the one component that arrives last in each, with its supplier and quoted lead time.
  2. For each of those parts, count your qualified sources and ask the supplier whether its capacity is on allocation and when its quoted lead time last changed.
  3. Where a part has one source and a growing lead time, start a second-source or make-vs-buy review now instead of waiting for the next shortage.
You are a procurement analyst finding the components that set lead time in our bills of material. Inputs, anonymized (codes instead of supplier and part names), one row per component: assembly code, component code, supplier code, quoted lead time (weeks), lead time 12 months ago (weeks), number of qualified sources, annual spend, and whether the supplier says capacity is on allocation (yes/no).
[PASTE TABLE]
1) For each assembly, name the component with the longest quoted lead time (the pacing part) and how many weeks longer it is than the next-longest component.
2) Flag pacing parts that have only one qualified source, a lead time up more than 25% in 12 months, or capacity on allocation.
3) Rank the flagged parts: single source first, then lead-time growth, then annual spend.
4) For the top five, suggest one next step each (second source, capacity reservation, buffer stock or make-vs-buy review) with a one-line reason.
Output: a table with columns assembly, pacing part, supplier, lead time (weeks), gap to next part (weeks), sources, 12-month change, flags, suggested step. Keep it under 300 words. Use only the data I give. List any missing or inconsistent data instead of guessing.

Tip of the DayFor Buyers

Have AI brief you before a tough supplier call

The hard part of a supplier call is remembering what happened across the last six emails while someone is talking. Paste the recent thread and your open issues, with supplier, part and people names swapped for codes, and ask your approved AI tool for a one-page brief: where things stand, what they'll likely push back on, and three questions to ask. It takes two minutes, and you stop remembering the key detail right after you hang up.

You are a purchasing analyst preparing a buyer for a supplier call. Inputs, anonymized (codes instead of supplier, part and people names): the call purpose, the last 3 to 6 emails or call notes, the open issues (late orders, price changes, quality problems) and the outcome I want.
[PASTE EMAILS AND OPEN ISSUES]
Call purpose: [PURPOSE]  Outcome I want: [OUTCOME]
1) Summarize where things stand in 3 bullets, with dates and quantities exactly as written.
2) List the open issues in priority order, with what was promised, by whom and by when.
3) Predict the supplier's two or three most likely objections or asks, based only on the emails.
4) Write three questions to ask and one fallback position if I don't get the outcome I want.
Output: a one-page brief with one heading per step. Keep it under 300 words. Use anonymized data only. List any missing or inconsistent data instead of guessing.

2. Infor lets planners and buyers approve ERP changes from inside Claude

Fix the purchase order without leaving the chat. Infor and Anthropic said on November 12 that select Infor customers can connect Claude to Infor's manufacturing systems, ask why a production order is at risk, and approve actions such as creating a purchase order or reallocating stock. Infor says every change needs the user's explicit approval, and existing Infor permissions decide who can see and do what.

So what for plannersIf your ERP vendor offers an AI assistant that can write back, decide which transactions planners may approve from chat, and test them on a copy of your data first.

3. project44 says its AI analyst got 10 of 10 test questions right, against 2 for a general model

Ten out of ten, against two out of ten. That's how project44 says its AI supply chain analyst, Mo, scored against a general-purpose large language model on verified test questions in its own internal testing, announced November 10 with a new Snowflake integration. The company credits customer-specific definitions, such as when on-time is measured from, and says 210 customers now use Mo; Forwarder News points out that project44 designed and ran the 10-question test itself.

So what for logistics leadsBefore you trust any AI analyst, give it 10 questions you already know the answers to, using your own on-time and detention definitions, and ask each one twice.

4. Freightos adds AI quoting agents to its platform for freight forwarders

Ready-to-send freight quotes in seconds. That's what Freightos says the AI agents in the new version of its air and ocean forwarder platform, launched November 10, can draft by reading customer requests and applying each client's procedures and margins. The platform connects to more than 75 air and ocean carriers, the company says, and Container News reports it is available to forwarders worldwide.

So what for buyersIf your forwarder starts sending AI-drafted quotes, check the first few against your contract rates and surcharge terms before you approve them.
